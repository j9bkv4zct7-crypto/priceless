CHAPTER TWO

Breath on Credit

PRICELESS · SEASON 1

Day two in a world where nothing is free.

Ren was still on the rooftop when it started, still sitting on the edge with his legs hanging over the dark. Pip had fallen asleep against his side about an hour ago, mid-sentence, mid-haggle, buried so deep in his old store jacket that only her cap and one hand showed. The hand was still holding the bun wrapper.

The Assayer sat a little apart, writing on her slate. Her name was Mirabel. She’d told him that somewhere in the second hour on the roof, grudgingly, the way other people hand over a coin. The loupe hung at her chest, and the crack he’d put in it ran right across the lens, a thin bright line.

Ren was looking up the sky-shaft. At the top of it, very far away, the real sky was going from black to grey.

There was still some light in him. He could feel it, faint and warm under his skin, a glimmer in the veins of his wrists when he turned them. What was left over from the jacket. He flexed his fingers and watched them shine.

He grinned.

“I’ve still got some left,” he said. “I’m saving it. Emergency fund.”

Mirabel didn’t look up.

“Are you,” she said.

High above them, the first ray of sunrise slipped over the lip of the shaft and began to slide down the stone toward his hand.

It touched his fingers, and the Grace went out like sparks dropped in water.

Fssshhhh.

A small, prim box hung in the air beside him.

GRACE EXPIRED · SUNRISE.

Ren stared at his hand. It was just a hand again. Dirty, a bit scraped, entirely ordinary.

“Hey!” he said. “I was saving that!”

Mirabel was writing.

“Rule,” she said. “Grace doesn’t keep overnight.”

He leaned over to look. On the slate, in small, neat handwriting, she had written: 1. Expires at sunrise.

On his chest, the tag ticked over with the dawn, like a clock turning a page.

AIR ACCOUNT: FRONTED · VEY, M. (29 DAYS)

Twenty-nine. Yesterday it had been thirty. Ren looked at the number for a while and decided not to do the math.

Pip’s home was a lean-to of crates and canvas wedged under a sweating pipe, so low Ren had to duck, so small that three people in it counted as a crowd. She had a pot on a little brazier, and in the pot was porridge, thin and grey, and she ladled it into three chipped bowls with the air of a hostess at a banquet.

Each bowl got a glyph as it filled. PORRIDGE · 1 MK.

“One mark a bowl,” Pip said. “Friend price.”

Ren looked at the bowl, then at her. “I gave you a melon bun yesterday.”

“And I sold you half back.” She sat down cross-legged. “We’re square.”

He couldn’t actually argue with that. He ate the porridge.

Mirabel unrolled the contract from the night before on the crate that served as a table and tapped its clauses one by one with her stylus.

“Terms,” she said. “I front your air for thirty days. In return, I observe. Every gift, you report it. Every test, you take it.”

“And if I don’t?”

“You stop breathing on day thirty.”

Ren swallowed his porridge. “…Great terms.”

A big shadow filled the doorway, along with a powerful smell of fish. The man from yesterday, the one with the awning, had found them. He had a meat hook in one hand, and he didn’t look like he’d come to share breakfast.

“Four marks,” said Sully the fishmonger.

He let it sit.

“Damages.“

Ren raised both hands.

“Okay,” he said. “Can I just… give you something? I’m good at that now.”

Mirabel didn’t even look up from her contract.

“You can’t give your way out of a debt,” she said. “A debt is a trade. Pay it in kind.”

Which was how Ren spent his morning hauling fish.

The crates were wet and heavy and full of things with eyes, and the ramp from Sully’s stall up to the cold-store was slick with a substance Ren decided not to identify. His tag glowed through his hoodie the whole way: DEBT: 4 MK. Every time he slipped, it seemed to glow a little brighter.

Pip trotted alongside him with a clipboard she’d produced from somewhere.

“That’s crate nine,” she called. “Sully, he’s on crate nine!”

“Ten crates, four marks!” Sully shouted back from below.

Pip leaned in close and whispered, like she was telling him a state secret, “I’m your agent. I get a cut.”

“Of four marks?”

“Of everything,” said Pip. “Forever.”

By the sixth crate she’d started hawking him to passers-by. “STRONG BACK!” she bellowed at a woman with a cart. “SLIGHTLY BROKE!”

That was the morning Ren learned how the Undercroft lived.

He saw it in pieces between crates. The breath-meter queues, longer than any queue he’d ever stood in on Earth, people in lines that bent around corners, each of them watching the little disc on their collarbone and the brass box on the wall with Thale’s crest on it, the scale and the closed eye. He saw how nobody talked in the queues, because talking took breath.

And he saw the posters.

Bailiffs were putting them up. They worked in pairs, one with a pot of paste, one with a stack of paper, and they slapped a poster on every pipe and every post down the whole long street. Ren didn’t understand what they were until he was close enough to see the drawing. It was bad. It was scratchy and rushed and somebody had given him far too much hair. But it was him. Under it, in big black glyph letters:

WANTED · 1,000,000 MARKS

People looked at the poster. Then they looked at Ren. Then at the poster again.

Some of them looked away fast, the way people do when they’ve seen something they’d rather not know. Others didn’t look away at all. There was one man, thin, in a dock-hand’s cap, who tore a poster right off a pipe and stood holding it in his fist, and his eyes stayed on Ren too long. They had a hungry shine in them.

Everyone down here owes someone, Ren thought, sliding the seventh crate onto the stack.

And now everyone knows what I’m worth.

There was a skinny kid behind a barrel near the cold-store. He wasn’t doing anything. He was just looking at the fish, the way you’d look at a fire on a cold night.

Ren saw him.

He didn’t think about it. He grabbed a fish off the top of the crate he was carrying and held it out.

“Here—”

The fish leapt out of his hand.

It didn’t fall; it leapt, snapping sideways through the air and smacking wetly back into the crate where it had come from.

SHLAP.

A glyph floated over the crate. NOT OWNED BY GIVER.

Sully, who had seen the whole thing, didn’t even blink.

“Thief and charity case,” he said. “Lovely.”

Mirabel had seen it too. Of course she had. She was writing it down. Ren saw the slate as he went past: 2. Must own it.

When the tenth crate was in, Sully dropped coins into Ren’s palm. Five of them. Then he plucked four of them back out again, one at a time, which felt unnecessarily theatrical. One mark stayed in Ren’s hand. Pip’s eyes locked onto it like a hawk’s onto a mouse.

“Five for the crates, four for the damages,” said Sully. “One left. Don’t spend it all on breathing.”

One mark.

Ren spun it over his knuckles, the way he used to do with a hundred-yen coin on slow nights at the register. He was good at it. He’d had a lot of slow nights. Then he went and knelt down in front of Pip.

“Pip,” he said. “Watch this.”

He let the coin dance across his fingers one more time.

“Grace, round two.”

Pip’s eyes were huge. “Ooooh.”

He flicked the coin into her palm with a flourish. He was already grinning at her, already waiting for the light, already imagining her face when it came.

“It’s free!”

Tink.

The coin landed in her palm.

Nothing happened.

No light. No warmth. No veins. Ren held his grin and waited, and the grin stayed where it was, but it started to feel heavier.

“…Any second,” he said.

Over his head, a small prim glyph began to assemble itself line by line, like a receipt printing.

TRADE DETECTED.

The receipt was big and very clear.

CONSIDERATION EXPECTED: ADMIRATION · 30 MK · OWED BY: VALUE-0 (REN)

His tag added a line underneath its others. DEBT: 30 MK.

“THIRTY?!”

Mirabel laughed.

It came out of her as a startled snort, the first time he’d heard her laugh at all. Pfft— She looked almost as surprised by it as he was. And then, before he could recover, before he could even close his mouth, she held out her palm toward him.

“Pfft— Test,” she said. “Give me something.”

Ren snatched up a pebble from the ground and offered it to her. He knew, even as he did it, that his face was doing something embarrassing. He was very obviously, very visibly hoping she’d approve.

CONSIDERATION EXPECTED: APPROVAL · 2 MK · OWED

Ren put his face down on the crate-table.

Donk.

Pip looked thrilled.

“So I was admiring you?” she said. “And that’s worth thirty marks?” She considered this. “I’m amazing.”

Mirabel had composed herself. She tapped her slate, and when she spoke it was in her Assayer voice again, crisp and level.

“The Assay reads intent. You wanted something back, a ‘wow,’ so it logged a sale.” She wrote. “Then it billed you for what you wanted.”

On the slate: 3. Want ANYTHING back = debt. On his chest: DEBT: 32 MK.

“So your power only works,” Mirabel said, “when you don’t care whether it works.”

Ren, still face-down on the table, said into the wood, “…That’s so stupid.”

It was. It was also, he suspected, exactly the kind of thing his grandmother would have found very funny.

He lifted his head. At the far end of the street, two Bailiffs had come round the corner. They were holding a poster up between them and comparing it to people’s faces, one face at a time.

“Scruffy,” one of them was saying. “Foreign. Brass tag on the chest.”

Before Ren could bolt, a big arm came out from under a stall counter and yanked him down.

Yoink.

He found himself crammed into the dark beneath the counter with his knees by his ears, next to the big stall-keeper from yesterday. Bertram. The one whose chit had snapped back. The man put a finger to his lips.

Boots went past. Two pairs. Slow.

Ren held his breath. So did Bertram. Ren could see the disc at the big man’s collarbone in the gloom, and he watched it not move. AIR · 41 BR … 41 … 41. Holding your breath down here wasn’t just a way of keeping quiet. It was a way of not paying.

The boots went on and faded.

Ren let his breath out. He whispered, “Thank y—”

“Don’t,” Bertram whispered back. “‘Thank you’ costs a mark.”

Ren thought about that.

“…Then I owe you.”

“You owe me a story.” Bertram nodded toward the street, toward the shack, toward Pip. “Same as the girl.” He crawled out from under the counter and straightened his apron. “Down here, that’s the only coin nobody’s taxed yet.”

They walked home at dusk through a crowded meter queue, Pip counting the day’s profit, which was one mark, over and over, as if it might breed.

“Minus my cut,” she said, “you’re worth nothing again. Congratulations.”

She glanced back over her shoulder. Just a glance, the kind of thing a kid does a hundred times a day. But her face changed.

Ren started to turn.

“Don’t look,” Pip said, low, without moving her head. “Walk normal.” He walked. “Normal, Ren, not that.”

He didn’t need to look. He’d seen enough in the corner of his eye: the thin man in the dock-hand’s cap, twenty steps behind them, the torn poster still crumpled in his fist.

They came to the mouth of a dark alley. And a hand came out of the crowd toward Ren’s collar from behind.

It wasn’t the man’s hand on Ren.

It was Pip’s, on the man’s sleeve.

She’d doubled back somehow, too fast to see, and planted herself in his path, small as she was, palm out, all business. Ren flattened himself into a doorway and stared.

“Looking for the Unpriced?” Pip said brightly. “I know where he sleeps. Directions are one mark. Courtesy tariff.”

The man hesitated. Then he fished out a coin.

DIRECTIONS · 1 MK · PAID.

Pip pointed, very confidently, down the wrong ramp, the one that led to the bailiffs’ post. The man hurried off.

Ren peeled himself out of the doorway.

“You sold me,” he said.

“I sold him.” Pip bit the coin to check it. “There’s a difference. I’m your agent.” She grinned up at him. “That’s two marks today. We’re rich.”

Night. The shack was dark except for the glow of Mirabel’s slate on the crate-table. Pip was asleep in the jacket, curled up like a cat. Ren sat with his back against a crate. Mirabel sat across from him, upright as ever, even now.

The slate’s rules glowed between them.

1. Sunrise. 2. Must own it. 3. No expectation. 4. Need matters (log: “CRITICAL” = ×).

Ren looked at it for a while.

“Why are you helping me?” he said.

“I’m not helping.” She didn’t look up. “I’m investing.”

“In what?”

“In finding out how much damage you can do.”

He’d half expected a joke. It wasn’t one. Then she did look up, straight at him, and there was something new in her face. Not suspicion. Curiosity. Real curiosity, the kind that wanted an actual answer.

“Why do you give, Ren?” she asked.

He thought of his grandmother at the kitchen door, two rice balls in a cloth.

“My grandma said what you give away is the only thing that’s really yours,” he said.

Mirabel went very still.

It was a strange stillness. Not shock, exactly. More like someone who has been walking on ice and has just heard it creak.

“My father said something like that once,” she said quietly. “Right before it ruined us.”

She stood and began to roll up the slate.

As she did, her coat sleeve fell back from her collar for a moment, and the disc at her collarbone caught the glow. Ren had never seen her whole tag before. She always kept her collar high. It had more lines than his did: her air, her rank, ASSAYER (JUNIOR) · SALTMARCH. And under those, a last line his eyes caught on before he could stop them.

HOUSE VEY DEBT: 212,000 MK (SHARE)

Two hundred and twelve thousand marks.

He’d spent the whole day earning one.

She didn’t notice him looking. Or she did and didn’t say. Ren didn’t say anything either. It wasn’t the kind of number you asked about.

“Sleep,” Mirabel said. “Breathing is expensive when you’re awake.”

He couldn’t sleep, so he went outside.

There was a kid by the meter on the corner, small, with bare knees, shivering in the bellows draft. He wasn’t looking at the meter. He was looking at Ren’s chest, at the badge pinned on his hoodie under the tag: the plastic name badge from the store, ASAMIYA, white letters on blue, catching the lamp.

“…’s pretty,” the kid said. His name, Ren found out later, was Dabble.

Ren looked down at the badge. It was a cheap piece of plastic that said his name. It had been on his chest every night for two years, and he’d never once thought it was pretty.

He unpinned it without a second thought.

“You collect shiny stuff?”

“Everything shiny costs,” said Dabble.

Ren pinned the badge on the kid’s shirt. Then he turned away, before the kid could even react. He didn’t wait to see his face. There was no flourish this time. He didn’t want one.

“Not this one,” he said.

Something glowed in his veins. Small and quiet. No receipt came.

UNCOMPENSATED TRANSFER · 1 BADGE.

Ren smiled to himself in the dark.

From the mouth of the alley came a cheery voice, and the clink of glass on glass.

“Lot nine! Delivery for lot nine!”

Old Hask stood in the alley mouth, grinning, his coat clinking with empty jars. The strange tag still floated over his head: NAME: — (SOLD). In one gloved hand he held up a sheet of parchment with a heavy gold seal at its foot.

“You ran off before the seal, you cheeky thing,” he said. “So I sealed it anyway.”

The deed glowed when Ren looked at it.

LOT 9 · MEMORY RIGHTS · SEALED · REMNANT HOUSE OF HASK

“My tag says no owner,” Ren said.

“Your body, sure.” Hask waved a hand. “The Assay’s cross about your body.” He tapped his own temple. “But your head? I paid for what’s in it.”

Ren’s fist clenched. The badge’s Grace was still in him, small and warm.

“Then I’ll break it,” he said, and slammed a Void Mark onto the deed.

Krak—

The Void Mark fizzled on the seal like a match on wet stone.

…fsst.

SEALED BY ASSAY · VOID MARK INSUFFICIENT.

Hask laughed, delighted, slapping his knee. “A badge’s worth of magic against an Assay seal! Oh, I like you.”

Running footsteps. Mirabel came round the corner at a run, breathing hard, and snatched the deed out of Hask’s hand without asking. Her eyes went down it line by line. Ren watched her face and saw the answer before she said it.

“…It’s valid.”

“Course it is,” said Hask, unoffended. “I’m a lot of things, Assayer. Sloppy isn’t one.”

Pip had woken. She peeked out of the shack, wrapped in the jacket, her hair sticking up under the cap.

“Who’s the jar man?”

Hask tapped one of the jars on his coat. It rang like a little bell.

“Extraction’s automatic,” he said. “No knives, no fuss. The Assay does it at sunrise.” He smiled at Ren, almost fondly. “Your oldest memory, three hundred marks. That’s my rent sorted.”

For the first time since the cash register, Ren was truly afraid.

“My oldest…” he said.

And there it was, before he could stop it, the way the oldest things come: blurred and warm and so faint it was almost not there. A man’s large hand resting on a small boy’s head. The weight of it. The warmth. The face above it was out of frame. It had always been out of frame.

Ren gripped the frame of the shack.

“What happens to it?”

“Goes in a jar,” Hask said. “You’ll never miss it. That’s the mercy of the thing.”

Mirabel stepped between them.

“I’ll buy the deed.”

Hask raised an eyebrow. “Three hundred marks?”

Ren watched her jaw tighten. He thought of the last line on her tag. Two hundred and twelve thousand. She couldn’t. She didn’t have to say so.

Pip whispered, horrified, “That’s a lot of porridge.”

Hask leaned in. He was kind about it. That made it worse.

“I paid for your memories, lad.”

A glyph clock appeared over Ren’s head, and it began to tick.

EXTRACTION · AT SUNRISE · 7 H 12 M

Its light lit his face from above. In the foreground, one of Hask’s empty jars glinted, waiting.

“The Assay,” said Hask, “extracts them at sunrise.”
