PRICELESS
Chapter One · Value: Zero

But before he ever woke beneath that stone ceiling, the world had already reached for him — through a cash-register drawer on an ordinary night, pulling him down into the gold.

CHAPTER ONE

Value: Zero

PRICELESS · SEASON 1

The sky was full of prices.

They hung over the market plaza as thick as stars, thousands of small glowing tags drifting in the dark: the price of a lamp, the price of a step, the price of a cough. Ren had stopped reading them a long time ago. You couldn’t live here and read every price. You’d never get anything else done.

Tonight they were all saying the same thing.

AIR · 0 BR.

It flashed red on the brass discs pinned at every collarbone in the plaza. Hundreds of people were on their knees on the cold stones, hands at their throats, mouths open like fish on a dock. The sound they made was small and wet and everywhere at once.

Hhk. Hkk.

At the edges of the plaza the Bailiffs were closing in, a ring of armor and long rattling price-chains, walking slowly, the way men walk when they know nobody can run.

Ren knelt in the middle of it all.

He could feel his own cheekbones when he swallowed. His lip had split days ago and kept splitting. The air around him was thin as paper, and every breath he dragged in came with a taste of brass. On his chest, over his heart, the tag fused into him glowed faintly, as if it were thinking.

“…okay,” he said. His voice came out cracked. “Okay okay okay.”

Beside him, an old man had fallen. A stranger. Ren didn’t know his name and had no time to ask it. The old man’s lips were blue, and his fingers clawed at the stones as if he could dig air out of them.

Ren took the old man’s head in both hands. He was careful about it, the way you’re careful with something that’s already breaking. He leaned down and pressed his forehead to the stranger’s. The skin was cold and damp.

His eyes were wet. His hands were steady.

“Take it,” he said.

Light broke out of the place where their foreheads touched.

It was white and gold and clean, the only clean thing in the plaza, and it came up out of Ren like water out of a split pipe. With it came a face. He felt it leave him before he saw it: an old woman, smiling, her eyes creased almost shut with it, half dissolving into the light as it rose. He knew that face better than his own. He knew the day he’d first seen it. He was giving that day away.

“The day I met my grandma,” he whispered, somewhere inside the light. “Her face. All of it.”

The light rolled outward across the plaza like a wave across a beach. Wherever it touched, the red tags flipped. AIR · 0 BR turned white, one after another, a field of red going pale. Bailiffs were thrown back off their feet, chains clattering on stone. And all around him, the kneeling crowd lifted their heads and breathed: one huge ragged breath, hundreds of chests rising together.

Every gift makes me poorer, Ren thought.

Every gift makes me stronger.

Saitama. 3:07 a.m.

The convenience store glowed alone on its dark street like the last lit window in the world. Rain had come and gone an hour ago and left the asphalt shining. Out front the vending machine hummed to itself, the steady bzzzz of something that would keep humming whether anyone came or not.

Inside, Ren Asamiya, twenty-four, scruffy-haired and healthy enough, a store-uniform jacket zipped over his grey hoodie, ran the handheld scanner over a tray of boxed lunches whose sell-by time had passed four minutes ago.

Beep.

The register screen blinked up at him. EXPIRED · DISCARD.

He glanced at the back office door. Closed. The manager’s light was on under it, but it was the kind of on that meant paperwork, not attention.

Ren slid two of the boxed lunches into a plastic bag. Then, after a second’s thought, a melon bun. The wrapper had a cartoon melon on it with a big stupid grin, and its price sticker, 128 yen, had been crossed through by a newer sticker that just said DISCARD.

Discard. Like that was a price.

Out back by the bins, Mr. Oda was sitting on his flattened cardboard. He was somewhere in his sixties, though it was hard to tell under the layers: three coats, maybe four, and a towel wound around his neck like a scarf. Ren crouched in front of him and held out the bag.

“Fresh-ish,” Ren said. “The chicken one’s still warm if you squint.”

Mr. Oda took the bag but didn’t look into it. He looked at Ren. “You’ll get fired one of these nights.”

“Probably.”

The old man rummaged in the bag, found the melon bun, and pulled it out. Then he reached over and pushed it firmly into the pocket of Ren’s jacket, the way you’d tuck a note into a child’s coat.

“That one’s yours,” he said. “You look thinner than me.”

“I’m on a diet.”

“You’re on minimum wage.”

Ren laughed, a real one that surprised him, and patted the pocket. The bun crinkled under his hand.

Behind him, the back door opened. Light fell into the alley, and a long shadow fell across both of them.

“Asamiya.”

The manager stood in the doorway with his tie loosened and the store’s waste log in one hand. He didn’t look angry. He looked tired, the kind of tired that has stopped being about one night.

“Third time this month,” he said.

“It was going in the trash.”

“Then the trash paid for it. That’s how it works.” The manager tapped the log against his palm. “Nothing’s free, Asamiya.”

At the edge of Ren’s vision, Mr. Oda was already staring at the ground and gathering himself up, cardboard and coats and all, to go.

Ren bowed. It came out low and stiff, like something that had rusted.

“Take it out of my pay,” he said.

The manager looked at him for a long second.

“…I will.”

Later, alone at the register, Ren stocked cigarettes into their slots. The fluorescent light over the counter flickered, caught, flickered again. He watched it without really seeing it.

“Nothing’s free…” he said, to nobody.

And there it was, the way it always came: a tiny kitchen in a tiny apartment, warm light, the smell of rice. He’d been five, maybe six. His grandmother at the door with two rice balls wrapped in a cloth, handing one of them out to the neighbor kid who always seemed to be around at dinnertime.

“What you give away is the only thing that’s ever really yours, Ren,” she’d said.

He’d frowned up at her with all the authority of a six-year-old. “That doesn’t make sense.”

She’d laughed. “It will when you’re broke.”

Ren smiled a little at the cigarette rack.

“Still broke, Grandma.”

He bought his own coffee at his own register, because that was how it worked. A hundred yen. He counted the coins out of a wallet so worn the leather had gone soft as cloth.

Clink. Clink.

When he opened it, the things behind his ID card looked back at him the way they always did. A photo of his grandmother. And a folded paper corner, soft at the creases from being unfolded too many times, with half a word printed on it in heavy black type: MISSING. Below that, a name. ASAMIYA KENJI.

His thumb stopped on the paper.

He didn’t think anything in particular. That was the trick of it, after nineteen years. You didn’t think anything. You just let your thumb stop there for a second, and then you closed the wallet.

He closed the wallet.

He sipped the coffee. It was bad and hot and exactly what he needed. Behind him, on the counter, the cash register’s drawer had begun to glow faintly at the seams, a thin gold line around its edges, and to hum, very softly.

Hmmmm.

He didn’t notice.

Ka-chunk.

The drawer slid open by itself.

Ren turned around. The drawer was open, all the way, which it never did without a key or a sale. Inside, the coins in their little trays were lifting. Rising into the air, rotating slowly as they rose, catching the light. As he watched, their edges blurred and their faces ran like wax, and then they weren’t coins anymore. They were shapes. Glowing gold shapes, like letters from an alphabet he didn’t know.

“…huh?” Ren said.

A box appeared in the air. It wasn’t on anything. It wasn’t coming from anywhere. It had jagged edges, like it had been torn out of something, and the words inside it weren’t spoken so much as pressed directly into him.

HOUSE THALE OF SALTMARCH: IN DEFAULT.

The gold glyphs came off the coins and onto him. They crawled up the arm he was holding his coffee with, a column of tiny lights moving like ants, warm and prickling and wrong. His fingers opened. The coffee cup fell and burst on the floor.

SEIZING COLLATERAL OF EQUAL VALUE.

“Wait wait wait—”

The drawer yawned.

There was no other word for it. It opened wider than a drawer could open, wider than the counter, deeper than the floor. Inside it was a well of golden light that went down forever. The glyphs on his arm pulled tight like a leash and yanked him forward, over the counter, into the drawer.

Shhlunk.

He fell.

He fell head over heels down a shaft that had no walls, only things. Coins, millions of them, spinning past in streams. Ledgers, thick as bricks, flapping their pages like wings. Contract scrolls unrolling as he passed, line after line of tiny script he couldn’t read, signatures, seals. And all around him, whirling like snow, the glyphs: price tags with nothing to hang on.

1 SWORD · 40 MK.

1 CHILDHOOD · 300 MK.

1 SUNRISE · LEASED.

His jacket flapped around him. His wallet had come halfway out of his pocket and was flapping open against his hip, and some stupid calm part of his brain was saying don’t lose the wallet, don’t lose the wallet, while the rest of him screamed.

“AAAAAAHH—”

The boxes came from everywhere at once, scattered through the falling coins like warning signs on a road.

APPRAISING…

APPRAISING…

APPRAISING…

And then black. Not darkness, exactly. A blankness, as if someone had shut a book on him.

In it, one small box.

…ERR—

Ren’s eyes snapped open.

“Hhh—!”

Darkness. Real darkness, the kind with cold air in it and the smell of iron and dust. He was lying on his back on a stone floor. His heart was going so hard he could feel it in his teeth.

He looked down at his chest.

There was a tag on it. A brass tag, palm-sized, with rounded corners, sitting over his heart. It had gone through his jacket. The cloth around it was scorched in a neat tag-shaped edge, and under it, where it should have been sitting on his hoodie, it was simply in him, fused there the way a scar is in you. The letters on its face were still cooling, orange fading into gold as he watched.

COLLATERAL · VALUE: 0 (UNAPPRAISABLE) · OWNER: HOUSE THALE

He grabbed it and pulled. It didn’t move. It was like pulling on his own sternum.

“No,” Ren said. “No, no, nope.”

When his eyes adjusted, he wished they hadn’t.

He was in a vault. That was the only word for it. Iron shelves ran up into the dark further than he could see, row after row of them, and every shelf was crowded with things, and every thing had a tag floating above it, small and glowing like the ones in the shaft.

A golden harp. 2,200 MK.

A child’s shoe, small and scuffed, one lace missing. 9 MK · SENTIMENTAL.

A sealed crate on a low shelf with a glass panel in its lid. And through the glass, a man. Sleeping, or something like sleeping. His face was slack and grey. Above the crate: 1 LABORER · 600 MK · 7 YRS.

Ren scrambled up and backed into a shelf so hard it rattled.

“Is this a— is that a person?”

At the sleeping man’s collarbone was a brass disc, like a coin pinned to his skin. A number glowed on it. AIR · BILLED TO HOUSE THALE.

Air. Billed.

Ren’s own hand drifted to his own collarbone. There was nothing there. Nothing but his hoodie and his skin. Whatever they were doing to the man in the crate, they were doing it to him through the thing on his chest.

Somewhere in the dark, iron groaned. A slit of light opened and widened, and the vault door swung back on a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

Grrrrnnn.

Silhouettes. Guards. And they were coming in.

There was a girl with them. A young maid, in a cap and apron, with a guard on either side of her. She came straight to Ren where he’d slid down against the shelf and offered him her hand, the most normal gesture in the world.

He took it.

As their palms met, a small glyph popped up between their hands like a bubble.

ASSISTANCE · 2 MK · BILLED TO: COLLATERAL ACCT.

“Up you get,” the maid said briskly. “Mind the shelf.”

Ren stared at the glyph, then at her. “…Did you just charge me for that?”

The maid looked genuinely confused. “Should I have charged you more?”

He didn’t have an answer to that. He didn’t think anyone did.

They walked him up out of the vault and into a corridor, and the corridor didn’t stop.

It was marble, white and veined with grey, and it ran ahead in a perfect straight line until perspective swallowed it. Every few meters, a brass box was mounted on the wall at chest height. Each box had a slot, like a mail slot, and a crest stamped on its face: a balance scale, its two pans level, above a closed eye.

The servants who passed them stopped at the boxes without thinking about it. A footman with a tray. A woman with an armful of linen. Each of them fed a little paper chit into a slot, and each of them, as the chit vanished, ticked. The discs at their collarbones changed. AIR · 88 BR. A chit. AIR · 288 BR.

Chk. Chk. Chk.

They were paying to breathe. All of them, all the time, in the middle of their work, without even looking. Ren touched his collarbone again. Nothing. His air, apparently, was being billed to House Thale. To the same people who owned the scale and the closed eye.

On the wall, in a gilt frame, hung a hand-lettered courtesy notice, the kind you’d find in a hotel.

HOUSEHOLD TARIFFS — “Thank you”: 1 mk · “Sorry”: 3 mk · Laughter in corridors: 5 mk

Ren read it twice. He decided not to laugh.

In an alcove off the corridor, a footman stood beside a bench where a woman sat propped against the wall. His wife; it was in the way he stood. She was pale, with sweat at her hairline. A doctor waited beside them with a bag, and beside the doctor stood someone Ren didn’t have a word for yet: a man in long gloves, with a bandolier across his chest, and in its loops, empty glass jars.

“Medicine’s eleven marks,” the doctor said. “Payment?”

The footman swallowed. Ren saw his throat move.

“The wedding,” the footman said. “Take the wedding.”

The man with the jars stepped forward and touched two gloved fingers to the footman’s temple. Something came out. A thread of light, soft and warm and loose at the edges, the color of old photographs and lamplight. It unspooled out of the footman like yarn from a sweater and wound itself into a jar. When the jar was full, Ren could see, faintly, inside it: a tiny chapel. Flowers. Two small figures.

A tag lit over the jar. 1 WEDDING DAY · 11 MK · SOLD.

Shhhhhhh.

The doctor handed over the medicine. The wife took it, and then she began to cry, quietly, holding on to her husband’s sleeve with both hands.

The footman looked down at her, politely puzzled.

“…Ma’am? Are you all right?”

Ren didn’t move. He couldn’t. Somewhere in his chest, under the tag, something had gone very cold and very still.

They brought him to a hall so big that the word hall felt like an insult to it.

Chandeliers hung from the ceiling in clusters, like bunches of crystal grapes. The floor was gilded, and inlaid in it, so large it took him a moment to see the whole shape, was a balance scale. The guards pushed him out to the center of it and left him standing on the crossbar, between the two great pans.

Above him, hanging from chains, was a ledger. An actual book, but the size of a house, open, its pages yellow and enormous. Lines of glyphs wrote themselves across it as he watched, in neat endless rows, without any hand to write them.

Heels clicked on the gold floor.

A young woman walked in. She was perhaps nineteen, slim and upright, in a severe high-collared coat whose trim was printed with ruled lines and tiny numerals, like the edges of a ledger. A brass loupe hung from a chain at her chest. In one hand she carried a small balance scale, no bigger than a teacup. Her hair was dark auburn and pulled back hard.

She didn’t look at his face. She looked at his tag.

“Junior Assayer Vey,” she said. “You’re the collateral.”

“I’m Ren.”

“Hold still, collateral.”

She raised the loupe to her eye.

Ren never saw what she saw through it. But he saw her see it.

He saw her eye, enormous behind the lens, move over him as if reading a page: his face, his chest, his hands. He saw her start to frown. He felt, rather than saw, something like a grid settle over him, cool and precise as a tape measure. And then he felt the grid bend.

Tik.

The loupe’s lens cracked. A single clean line across the glass.

In her other hand, the little scale began to spin. Not tip. Spin, its pans whirling around their post like a compass needle gone mad.

Whirrrrrr.

The Assayer lowered the loupe. For half a second, no more, she looked unnerved. Then her face closed over it, smooth as a lid.

“Zero isn’t a price,” she said. “Zero is an error.”

A voice came down from above them, warm and amused, the voice of a man who has just been told a mildly good joke.

“Then he is worthless.”

Everyone in the hall stiffened. Every servant, every guard, the Assayer herself, all of them bowed in one smooth movement, as though they’d been strung on the same wire.

He came down the curving staircase as if he had all the time in the world, because he did.

He was in his fifties, tall and slim, with dark hair swept straight back and silver at the temples. He was beautifully dressed, in the kind of dark tailored coat that makes everyone else in a room look like they got dressed in the dark. He carried a teacup in one hand. On his collar, in gold, the crest: the scale above the closed eye. Behind him the huge ledger went on writing.

Ren noticed, without wanting to, that the man had no disc at his collarbone. No tag. No number. He didn’t need one.

“I owed the Assay four hundred thousand marks,” the man said pleasantly, as though resuming a conversation. “And it sent me you.”

He reached the floor and began to walk around Ren in a slow circle, his eyes moving over him the way people’s eyes move over a horse at a fair. Teeth, legs, temper.

“Look, sir,” Ren said, “there’s been a mix-up. I need to go home. I have a shift Thursday.”

The man stopped in front of him. When he spoke, he spoke gently, which was worse.

“‘Home’ has been paid, boy. You are what it paid with.”

The man sipped his tea and looked over the rim of the cup at the Assayer.

“Can he be priced, Vey?”

A tiny hesitation. “…No, my lord.”

“Worthless, then.” He was already turning away, already bored. “Sell him to the Remnant Houses. Earth memories fetch a curiosity price.”

The guards took Ren by the arms.

As they did, he caught the Assayer, Vey, looking back at him. Her face gave away nothing. But she was looking.

Then they were dragging him backward across the inlaid scale, and his sneakers were squeaking on the gold, and the word was going around and around in his head.

“Memories? Wait— what do you mean memories—!”

The auction hall sat on a high terrace of the city. Ren had a view, which felt like a joke. Through the tall window behind the auctioneer’s podium he could see the city falling away in tiers, terrace under terrace, roof under roof, down into a fog so thick it looked solid.

Rows of buyers sat in front of him in fur collars. He stood on a wooden block with his wrists chained in front of him.

“Lot nine!” the auctioneer called. “Collateral, foreign, unappraised! Do I hear ten marks?”

Silence.

Somebody in the third row coughed. A tiny glyph ticked off the collarbone disc of the woman who’d done it. COUGH · 0.2 MK.

Ren had never in his life wanted so badly to be worth ten marks.

At the back of the room, a hand went up. It was thin and spotted with age and wrapped in a fingerless glove.

“Forty,” said a voice.

The man who owned the hand was in his seventies and grinning like he’d found a coin on the street. His coat had more pockets than Ren had ever seen on a single garment, and every pocket clinked, because every pocket held an empty glass jar. The tag floating over his head was strange. Where everyone else’s tags had a number, his said:

NAME: — (SOLD)

“I collect the rare ones,” the old man said.

“Sold,” cried the auctioneer, “to the Remnant House of… uh… Mister… Hask!”

Ren felt the tag on his chest shift. He looked down. The letters were rewriting themselves.

OWNER: HASK (PENDING SEAL)

Hask’s handcart rattled along an open walkway with Ren chained in the back of it like a sack of potatoes. The walkway hugged the side of the terrace. The railing beside it was waist-high and nothing more, and beyond it there was nothing at all, just fog going down and down.

“Your first heartbreak alone’ll pay my rent,” Hask said cheerfully, over his shoulder.

Ren was sweating. “Cool. Great. Love that.”

He was looking at the railing. He was looking at it very hard.

The cart hit a bump. Something clanked behind him, and the chain on his wrists slid free of the hook it had been looped over. He looked at the hook. He looked at the fog.

This is a terrible idea, thought a very reasonable part of his mind.

The rest of him was already moving. He got one foot on the side of the cart and vaulted the railing, out into the white.

“Oi—!” Hask yelled.

“NOPE—!” Ren yelled back, and fell.

Below the fog, there was a city.

Ren saw it in pieces as he dropped through the last of the white: a stone sky, a cavern roof so high and so wide it might as well have been weather; and hanging under it, a hive. Shacks stacked on shacks. Walkways strung between them like washing lines. Pipes everywhere, fat and thin, sweating and dripping, running in every direction at once. Lamps burned in a thousand windows with a sick yellow light.

In the middle of it all, bolted to the cavern roof, were the lungs.

There was no other way to see them. Enormous brass bellows, ribbed and riveted, each one the size of a building, slowly heaving. They drew in, and the whole cavern seemed to lean toward them. They pushed out, and warm stale air rolled down over the shacks like a sigh.

HWOOOOOOH… HWOOOOOOH…

Every one of them carried the crest: the scale, the closed eye. And beside each crest hung a price glyph as big as a house.

AIR · 3 MK / 100 BR.

Below them, long queues of people snaked toward brass meters on the walls. And over everything, over every shack and stall and pipe and person, the tags hung like laundry.

The Undercroft.

Ren had about a second and a half to take all this in before he hit a canvas awning, went straight through it, and landed on something wet.

Krrssh. Thud.

The something wet was fish.

Ren untangled himself from the torn canvas, the fish, and the crate the fish had been in. The awning’s owner was standing over him: a broad man in a stained apron, smelling powerfully of the sea, holding out one open palm. Above the palm, a glyph floated up as calmly as a bubble.

DAMAGES · 4 MK.

“I— don’t have—” Ren started.

“Then you owe,” said the fishmonger.

Ren didn’t wait to find out what owing meant down here. He ducked under the man’s arm and hurried off into the crowd, still dripping, still chained at the wrists, the tag on his chest glowing through his jacket for anyone to read.

He passed a wall sign, painted in neat official letters.

COURTESY TARIFFS · By order of House Thale — Directions: 1 mk · Holding a door: 2 mk · Listening (per min): 3 mk · Comfort (crying): 5 mk · A sincere apology: 8 mk

He saw a small boy, red-eyed, hand a coin to a woman. The woman took the coin and patted the boy’s head exactly three times. Not two, not four. Three. Then she walked away.

COMFORT · 5 MK · PAID.

At the meter queue, a man doubled over and went down on his knees, wheezing, his face going a terrible color. At the last possible second, with his fingers shaking, he shoved a chit into the slot. His air came back to him in one huge gulp.

Chk— HAAAH.

Nobody in the queue looked at him. They were all watching their own discs.

Ren stopped walking.

Through the crowd ahead of him, a girl was moving along a wall. She was small, maybe nine, in a cap so big it came down over her ears and a shirt that was more patches than shirt. She wasn’t walking so much as staggering, one hand on the wall, the other at her throat.

Her lips were blue.

Hk. Hk.

The disc at her collarbone was different from everyone else’s. A chain of glowing glyphs was wrapped around it, tight, like a padlock locked through a ring. The words on it pulsed red.

AIR LICENSE: REVOKED · LOCKED.

People were stepping around her. That was the thing Ren would remember later. Not that they hurt her, but that they stepped around her, eyes down, careful not to brush her, the way you’d step around a puddle. A woman with a child by the hand saw her coming and pulled the child out of the way.

Then a stall-keeper came out from behind his counter. He was a big man, heavyset, with a face that looked like it had been made for laughing and hadn’t been allowed to for a long time. He couldn’t stand it. Ren could see on his face that he couldn’t stand it. He knelt beside the girl in the street and held out a breath chit in a trembling hand.

“Here, girl,” he said. “Just take it—”

SNAP!

The chit leapt back into his palm as if it were on a spring. His whole hand jerked with it.

TRANSFER REFUSED · NO CONSIDERATION.

A second glyph bloomed over the stall-keeper’s head, red and tidy.

ATTEMPTED GIFT · FINE 10 MK.

The big man stared at his palm. The chit sat there, his again, as if it had never left. Something went out of his face that Ren thought might have been the last of something.

The onlookers murmured.

“The Exchange refuses,” someone said.

“Don’t, Bertram,” someone else said. “You’ll be short-breathed too.”

Ren stood in the crowd with his eyes wide and his mouth open.

“…it won’t let him?” he said, very quietly.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. He’d seen it.

He shoved his way through. He didn’t decide to. His body just did it, the way it had gone over the railing. He knelt by the girl on the stones.

“Hey,” he said. “Hey, look at me. Breathe.”

She looked at him. Even choking, even blue around the mouth, she managed to glare.

“Can’t…” she got out. “Pay you…”

“I don’t want anything.”

“Liar.” Her eyes were sharp and furious and wet. “Everybody wants… something.”

That landed somewhere Ren didn’t expect. He opened his mouth and nothing came out for a second.

“…I want you to breathe,” he said.

He dug in his pockets.

His wallet first. He pulled it out, and a glyph sizzled over it like fat in a pan. FOREIGN CURRENCY · NO VALUE.

His phone. The screen was black and dead, a little slab of glass. NO VALUE.

Lint. No glyph at all. Even lint here had more dignity than to be priced.

Then his hand closed on something soft in his jacket pocket. Something that crinkled.

He pulled it out. The melon bun, squashed flat from the fall, from the fish, from everything, its cartoon melon still grinning up at him from the wrapper as if nothing had happened. He waited for the glyph. No glyph came. Nothing hung over it at all. It was just a bun. His bun. Given to him and owned outright.

“…Mr. Oda,” Ren said softly.

He tore the wrapper open. Crumbs went everywhere.

Krrsh.

The girl’s nose twitched. Half-conscious, she smelled it, sweet and stale and bready, and her eyes flickered.

Ren held the squashed bun out to her in both hands. He didn’t think about how he was doing it. If he’d thought about it, he’d have recognized it. It was the way his grandmother had held out a rice ball at a kitchen door, a long time ago, in another world.

“Here,” Ren said. “It’s free.”

The bun crossed the space between his fingers and hers. Her fingertips came up to meet it. The street went silent.

Her fingers closed on the bun.

Every price on the street broke at once.

The tags warped. They stuttered. They tore into static, as if the world were a screen and someone had kicked it. The fishmonger’s DAMAGES tag. The courtesy sign on the wall. The meter readouts, every one of them. Far overhead, even the house-sized glyph beside the nearest Great Bellows flickered, and for one second it didn’t say AIR · 3 MK / 100 BR. It said ERR.

They all said ERR. ERR. ERR.

Pale gold light spilled up from the bun, up between their hands, bright enough to make Ren squint. And across the whole sky of the Undercroft, a box tore itself open, jagged and enormous and screaming.

UNCOMPENSATED TRANSFER

ERROR · ERROR · ERROR · ERROR

KZZZZZRRRRRTT.

The light didn’t stay in the bun.

It came into him.

Ren arched back on his knees as it hit. White-gold, it raced up his arms in branching lines, under his skin, like lightning running along the veins. His eyes flared with it. He could feel it in his teeth and his fingertips and the backs of his eyes, warm, enormous, full.

VMMMMMMM.

“Hh— HHAAH—!”

On his chest, the tag stuttered.

VALUE: 0 … 0 … 0 …

And somewhere, very small, the voice with no source said:

…RECALCULATING.

Ren looked at his glowing hands. He was terrified. He was also, under the terror, more alive than he’d ever been in his life, and it scared him nearly as much.

“What…” he said. “Is…”

When he looked back at the girl, he could see it. Really see it, the way you suddenly see the pattern in a picture once someone points it out. The glyph-chain wrapped around her breath disc wasn’t just words. It was a lock. A real one, made of light and law, red and pulsing, and it was tightening like a collar.

She had the bun in both hands. She hadn’t been able to eat it. She gagged, out of air.

“Kh—”

Ren didn’t think. His glowing palm reached for the lock on her tag.

His palm slapped down on it.

A circle of light stamped itself over the tag, like the seal of a rubber stamp: the outline of a tag, with a single slash straight through it. A mark that meant no.

The red chain shattered. It burst into shards, glyph-fragments spinning away into the air, broken words still flickering as they fell.

AIR LIC— REV— LOC—

KRAAAANG.

The girl inhaled.

It was the biggest breath Ren had ever heard anyone take. It went on and on, a huge desperate full-chested gulp of air that seemed to pull half the street into her, and as it went in, her color came back. Blue to pink, grey to living, like watching a drawing get colored in.

HHHHHHAAAAAAAAAH.

All around them, the crowd stood stunned. Bertram the stall-keeper’s fingers opened, and his chit fell to the stones.

The girl coughed. She breathed. She coughed again and breathed again, and then she did the thing Ren would remember for the rest of his life: she bit the melon bun. She was crying and chewing at the same time, her cheeks streaked, her mouth full.

“Mmf,” she said. “…’s sweet…”

Ren laughed. It came out shaky and cracked, and he sat back on his heels because his legs had stopped being reliable. The light in his veins dimmed a little, settling, like a fire banking down.

“Ha… haha… okay.” He wiped his face with a chained wrist. “Okay, what.”

All around them, the street’s glyphs were flickering back to normal, one by one, ERR giving way to prices again: the damages, the tariffs, the meters. People stared. At him. At the girl. At the shards of her lock, still drifting in the air like the last sparks of a firework.

“He gave—”

“It let him—?”

“What is he—”

Then a long shadow fell over Ren and the girl, and the crowd melted away from it like snow off a stove.

A voice spoke, flat and close.

“An unpriced transfer…”

The man standing over them was in his thirties, and he looked like he’d been tired for most of them. Heavy greatcoat, with the crest on it. Stubble. Eyes that had looked at a great many people on a great many bad days. And around both of his arms, wound like rope around a post, were chains: long price-chains strung with tiny brass tags, dozens of them, that rattled against each other when he moved, like wind chimes.

“…is a crime against the Scale,” he finished.

Chink. Chink.

The girl scrambled behind Ren so fast she nearly knocked him over.

“Bailiff…!” she hissed.

The Bailiff read Ren’s chest tag without bending down to do it.

“‘Owner: Hask, pending,’” he said. “So you’re stolen goods.”

His eyes moved to the girl behind Ren.

“And she’s breathing air nobody paid for.”

He flicked his wrist. The chains came off his arms like snakes off a branch.

SHRAKKK.

They coiled around Ren’s torso and legs and yanked him flat onto the stones. The air went out of him. On every link, the little brass tags flared at once, all saying the same thing.

LIEN · LIEN · LIEN.

“GHK—!”

Ren strained against them. He reached for the light, the way you’d reach for a word you know, and he felt it come: a faint glow in his veins, sputtering, flickering—

And dying. Like a match in the wind.

Fssst…

“Whatever you did,” said the Bailiff, “you spent it.”

He turned to the girl. He wasn’t cruel about it. That was the worst part. He was just procedural, like a man reading out a form.

“Unlicensed air gets repaid, girl. With interest.”

“I didn’t ask for it—”

“Nobody asks.”

Ren lay pinned with his cheek on the cold stone and gritted his teeth.

Nothing left, he thought. I’ve got nothing left.

A cold draft swept down the street from the Great Bellows, a long stale breath of it. Hwoooooh… The girl shivered in her thin patched shirt, hugging herself, her lips still pale.

Ren looked down at himself. At the cheap store jacket, scorched around the tag. At the name badge still pinned to it, ASAMIYA in plastic letters, absurd and bright.

His chained hand began to inch, very slowly, toward the zipper.

The Bailiff yanked the chain, and Ren was dragged a meter across the stones, scraping.

“Stay down,” the Bailiff said.

Ren got the zipper down anyway, inch by inch, tooth by tooth.

Zzzzip.

“Pip,” he said. He didn’t know how he knew her name. Somebody in the crowd must have said it. “Pull.”

“What—?”

“The sleeve. Pull.“

She grabbed his sleeve and hauled. The jacket slid out from under the chains like a skin being shed. The brass tag stayed where it was, fused through, so that the jacket came away with a small, scorched, tag-shaped hole over its left breast. The links bit down tighter on the thin grey hoodie underneath.

Pip clutched the jacket to her chest, bewildered.

“What…” she said. “What d’you want for it?”

Ren, flat on the ground, chained, with a bailiff’s boot by his ear, smiled at her.

“For you to be warm,” he said.

She hesitated. Then she pulled the jacket around her shoulders. It swallowed her. The hem came down past her knees, and the sleeves hung a foot past her hands.

And in Ren’s eyes, the light caught again.

UNCOMPENSATED TRANS—

Grace exploded out of him.

It came up through his chest and down his arms and out of every part of him at once, and the chains burst. They didn’t break; they burst, link by link, all the way along, and their little LIEN tags popped like firecrackers. The Bailiff was thrown backward into a breath meter on the wall, and the meter burst open, and a blizzard of paper chits sprayed out of it into the air.

BWAKKOOOOM.

Fwap. Fwap. Fwap.

The Bailiff got up slowly. Chits drifted down around him like leaves. He looked at Ren. Then he looked at Pip, small and swallowed up in the blue store jacket. Something crossed his face that Ren couldn’t read.

He began to wind up his broken chains. He turned away.

“This isn’t finished,” he said. “Everything gets paid.”

Ren sat up, grinning, the light fading out of his veins.

“Ha…” he said. “Did you see that? Did you—”

He coughed.

Khh.

He looked down at his chest. The tag was rewriting itself again.

OWNER: NONE · AIR ACCOUNT: UNFUNDED.

The air around him went thin. It was hard to describe, later. It was as though the world itself had stepped back from him, as if the street had fewer lines in it, fewer details, as if everything were fading at the edges. He put his hands to his throat. The breath he tried to pull in came in like a thread instead of a stream.

Pip panicked.

“Hey— HEY—!”

Ren crumpled onto the stones. The last thing he saw, at the edge of everything, was a pair of polished boots stepping into view.

“Breathing’s expensive down here,” said Junior Assayer Vey.

She was crouching beside him with a slim slate open on her knee. The hem of her severe coat was grey with dust. She’d followed him. All the way down from the estate, through the fog, to this street.

Pip planted herself in front of Ren with her arms spread wide, the jacket sleeves flapping.

“Back off, Assayer!”

The Assayer ignored her. She turned the slate so that Ren could see it. On its surface, in the same glowing script the tags used, was a log.

COLLATERAL · TRANSFER: 1 MELON BUN · RECIPIENT NEED: CRITICAL · VALUE 0 → 4,100 MK (GRACE)

TRANSFER: 1 JACKET · VALUE 0 → 2,600 MK (GRACE)

“Nobody here gives,” she said quietly. She said it the way other people say a prayer, or a curse. “Nobody can. Not in three hundred years.” Her grey eyes held his. “Do you understand what you just did?”

Ren lay on his back, wheezing. He thought about it.

“…fed a kid?”

She unrolled a short contract scroll. The seal at its bottom was already inked, as though she’d written the thing on the way down.

“I’ll front your air,” she said. “Thirty days.”

“For… what?”

“Exclusive rights to observe you.”

Ren waited for the rest of it. There wasn’t any.

“…That’s it?”

“You own nothing else,” said the Assayer. “I checked.”

She held out a stylus. Ren took it in a shaking hand and signed where she pointed. He wrote REN, sloppy and in English, the letters wobbling across the line.

Skritch.

The seal glowed. His tag updated with a small, almost polite chime.

AIR ACCOUNT: FRONTED · VEY, M. (30 DAYS)

The air came back.

Ren took a huge breath. Then another. Then he lay flat on the stones and laughed up at the stone sky, laughed until his ribs hurt.

HHHAAAAHH.

When he stopped, Vey was standing over him, rolling up the contract. She wasn’t smiling.

“You’re the most dangerous thing in this city,” she said.

Ren, still flat on his back, raised one hand like a student in a classroom.

“I’m the poorest thing in this city.”

The corner of her mouth twitched. Almost a smile. Almost.

Pip, swamped to the knees in his jacket, raised her hand too.

“Can I be dangerous too?”

“No,” said the Assayer.

Far above the Undercroft, past the Great Bellows, up through the pipes and the fog, past terrace after terrace of the high city, a single tower stood at the very top of Saltmarch with one lit window.

The room behind that window was round, and its walls were a book.

Floor to ceiling, all the way around, the walls were the pages of a single enormous living ledger, and across them, in neat endless rows, glyph-lines wrote themselves without any hand. Night pressed against the tall windows. At a small table in the middle of the room sat the Margrave Oswin Thale, with a fresh cup of tea.

A young clerk hurried in, clutching a candle, sweating through his collar.

“My lord. The Ledger—”

“Is the Ledger,” said Thale, without looking up. “It does not need announcing, Wendle.”

The clerk’s hand was shaking as he lifted it and pointed. Thale turned his head toward the wall.

“It’s moving the wrong way,” said the clerk.

Every line on every page had the same small new mark.

Bread. Rent. Air. Comfort. Memories. Every price, in every row, all the way around the room and up to the ceiling. The numbers trembled, like reflections on water. And beside each one, small and new:

−0.01% · −0.01% · −0.01%

For the first time in three hundred years, every price in Saltmarch had gone down.

Thale set his teacup down on its saucer. He set it down too hard. A hairline crack ran up the porcelain.

Tk.

Thale rose. He walked to the wall and laid two fingers on a line of glyphs, gently, the way a doctor might take a pulse.

“Someone gave something away,” he said quietly.

The door opened. The Bailiff came in, his coat torn, a few paper chits still stuck to the wool. He bowed.

“The Undercroft, my lord. A boy. Foreign.” He straightened. “Your collateral.”

A slow, interested smile spread over Thale’s face.

“The worthless one.”

Thale walked a slow circle around the Bailiff, his hands clasped behind his back.

“Do you know why we price things, Gray?”

“…To keep accounts, my lord.”

Thale stopped at the window and looked down. Beneath him, the city’s tiers fell away into the dark, ring under ring, down to where the Undercroft’s sickly glow showed through the fog like a bruise.

“To remember them,” he said. “What’s free is worthless. What’s worthless is forgotten.”

He let that sit.

“And a world that forgets its prices forgets us.”

He turned back from the window.

“Find it,” he said. “Price it.”

He took a writ from the clerk’s tray and signed it with one long, elegant stroke.

“And if it cannot be priced…”

He pressed his crest-ring into the wax. The seal glowed gold.

“…make it worthless.”

Chnk.

The writ dissolved in his hand. In its place was a single burning glyph, and the glyph streaked out of the open window like a struck match.

FSSSHHOOOO.

From outside, if anyone had been there to watch, it would have looked like a falling star: a line of gold arcing over the high city, over the terraces and the fog, and diving down toward the Undercroft below.

There was a rooftop on the Undercroft’s rim where a narrow shaft opened in the stone sky, and far, far up it, so far it looked like the bottom of a well seen from the wrong end, was the real sky. Thin pre-dawn light came down it, grey and cool and clean.

Ren sat on the edge of the roof with his legs dangling over nothing. He was in his hoodie now, only his hoodie, and he’d moved the ASAMIYA badge off the jacket and pinned it to his chest, just below the tag, because it seemed like the kind of thing a person should keep. Pip sat beside him, still swamped in the jacket, holding the last half of the melon bun in both hands as if it were treasure. The Assayer, Vey, stood a little apart from them, writing in her slate.

“Half for a story,” Pip said.

Ren looked at her. “You know I gave you the whole thing, right?”

“And now I’m selling half.” She held it up. “Fair trade.”

Ren grinned. He couldn’t help it.

“…Deal,” he said. “Once upon a time, there was a convenience store—”

“A what?” said Vey, without looking up.

Something glinted, high up in the sky-shaft above them. A spark of gold, falling.

If you’d pulled back, far enough, you’d have seen three tiny figures on a rooftop at the edge of a vast cavern. Under them, the Great Bellows breathing, slow and heavy. And below that, spread out across the whole Undercroft, a million price tags glittering in the dark like a sea of stars.

The burning glyph came down the shaft and struck Ren square in the chest tag, in a small burst of gold.

He felt it like a coin dropped into a slot. The tag rewrote itself, big and clean.

VALUE: 0 · BOUNTY: 1,000,000 MARKS

Ting.

Ren looked down at the new number, lit from above by the thin light of a sky he couldn’t reach. He grinned a broke man’s grin.

“Everything here has a price,” he said.

“Mine just went up.”
